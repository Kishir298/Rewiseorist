# The Industrial Revolution

## Overview

The **Industrial Revolution** was a period of rapid industrial change that began in Britain around 1750 and continued into the 1900s. It describes the shift from making goods by hand in homes and small workshops to making them with machines in large factories. The change was not instant, and different industries and regions industrialised at different speeds.

## Causes

Several developments made industrial growth possible. Improvements in farming, including crop rotation, selective breeding and enclosure, produced more food and supported a growing population. At the same time, enclosure and changes in farming reduced the need for some rural workers, who moved to towns looking for work.

Britain had large supplies of **coal** and **iron ore**, the raw materials needed for steam power and machinery. Canals and, later, railways moved heavy goods more cheaply. Trade with the empire provided markets and raw materials such as cotton. Banks and investors supplied capital for factories, mines and transport.

## Textile inventions

The cotton industry changed first. The **flying shuttle** (1733) made weaving faster. The **spinning jenny** (c. 1764) allowed one worker to spin several threads at once. The **water frame** (1769) used water power to produce stronger thread and encouraged the building of factories beside rivers. The **power loom** (1785) used water or steam power to weave cloth mechanically. Together these inventions increased output but also displaced some skilled hand-workers.

## Steam and railways

Early steam engines were used to pump water out of mines. **James Watt** improved the steam engine in the 1760s and 1770s, making it more efficient and useful for driving machinery. Steam power freed factories from relying on rivers.

Railways transformed transport. **George Stephenson's Rocket** won a famous competition in 1829, and the Liverpool to Manchester railway opened in 1830. By the 1840s, railways linked major cities. Journeys became faster, goods could be sold in distant markets, and fresh food and newspapers travelled more widely. Railway building also created jobs and encouraged a standard national time.

## Factories, towns and reform

The **factory system** brought workers and machines together under one roof. Factory owners wanted long hours and low costs. Workers faced dangerous machinery, poor lighting and little protection. Many children worked in mills and mines because they were cheap and small enough to crawl under machines.

Towns grew quickly as people moved to find work. This **urbanisation** often led to overcrowded housing, dirty streets and polluted water. Diseases such as cholera spread. Reformers campaigned for change. The **Factory Act of 1833** appointed inspectors and limited the hours of child workers. Later laws reduced working hours for women and children, and the **Public Health Act of 1848** encouraged towns to improve sanitation.

## Key facts

- The Industrial Revolution was a gradual shift from hand production to machine-powered factory production, beginning in Britain around 1750.
- Coal, iron, capital, colonial trade and improved transport supported industrial growth.
- Inventions such as the spinning jenny and power loom increased textile output.
- Steam engines and railways changed manufacturing, transport and the pace of trade.
- Factory work and rapid urbanisation created overcrowding, disease and poor working conditions, prompting reform laws.
